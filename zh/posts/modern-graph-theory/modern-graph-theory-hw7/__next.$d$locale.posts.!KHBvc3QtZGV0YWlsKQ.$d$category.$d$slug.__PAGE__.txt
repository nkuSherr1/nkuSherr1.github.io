$$

### Homework

**Problem 1.**

Prove: Every $n$-vertex graph with at least $2ex(n,C_4)$ edges contains at least $(\frac{3}{2}-o(1))n^2$ many copies of $C_4$.

**Proof.**

Let $G$ be an $n$-vertex graph with $m=e(G)\ge 2ex(n,C_4)$. For distinct vertices $x,y$, we note

$$
N_{xy}:=|N(x)\cap N(y)|.
$$

Define a **labeled fork** to be an ordered triple $(x,z,y)$ with $x\neq y$ and $xz,zy\in E(G)$. Let $F$ be the number of labeled forks.

Counting by the center $z$:

$$
F=\sum_{z\in V(G)} d(z)(d(z)-1).
$$

By Jensen Inequality,

$$
F\ge n\left(\frac{2m}{n}\right)\left(\frac{2m}{n}-1\right)=\frac{4m^2}{n}-2m.
$$

By Theorem 1, we get

$$
m\ge 2ex(n,C_4)=(1+o(1))n^{3/2},
$$

hence

$$
F\ge (4-o(1))n^2.
$$

Counting forks by endpoints:

$$
F=\sum_{x\neq y} N_{xy}=2\sum_{\{x,y\}\in \binom{V}{2}} N_{xy},
$$

so

$$
\sum_{\{x,y\}} N_{xy}\ge (2-o(1))n^2.
$$

Let $N=\binom{n}{2}=(\frac12+o(1))n^2$. By convexity of $u\mapsto \binom{u}{2}$,

$$
\sum_{\{x,y\}}\binom{N_{xy}}{2}
\ge
N\binom{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\{x,y\}}N_{xy}}{2}
\ge
N\binom{4-o(1)}{2}
=(3-o(1))n^2.
$$

Now $\sum_{\{x,y\}}\binom{N_{xy}}{2}$ counts each $C_4$ exactly twice (once for each pair of opposite vertices). Therefore

$$
\#C_4(G)\ge \frac12(3-o(1))n^2
=\left(\frac32-o(1)\right)n^2.
$$

∎

**Problem 2.**

Prove: Every set of $n$ points in the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ has at most $O(n^{3/2})$ unit distances.

**Proof.**

Let $P$ be the $n$-point set, and build a graph $G$ on vertex set $P$ by joining two points iff their Euclidean distance is $1$. Then $e(G)$ is exactly the number of unit distances.

We claim $G$ is $K_{2,3}$-free. Indeed, if two points $a,b$ had three common neighbors $x_1,x_2,x_3$, then each $x_i$ would lie on both unit circles centered at $a$ and $b$. But two distinct circles intersect in at most two points, contradiction.

Hence $e(G)\le ex(n,K_{2,3})$. By Theorem 2,

$$
ex(n,K_{2,3})\le (3-1)^{1/2}n^{3/2}+(2-1)n=\sqrt{2}\,n^{3/2}+n=O(n^{3/2}).
$$

Therefore the number of unit distances is $O(n^{3/2})$.

∎

**Problem 3.**

Prove: For fixed $k\ge 2$, there exists a constant $c_k$ such that

$$
ex\!\left(n,\mathcal{C}_{\le 2k}^{e}\right)\le c_k n^{1+1/k},
\qquad
\mathcal{C}_{\le 2k}^{e}=\{C_4,C_6,\dots,C_{2k}\}.
$$

**Proof.**

Let $G$ be an $n$-vertex graph with no even cycle of length at most $2k$, and let $m=e(G)$.

Take a bipartite subgraph $H\subseteq G$ with at least $m/2$ edges. Then $H$ is still $\mathcal{C}_{\le 2k}^{e}$-free. Since $H$ is bipartite, all its cycles are even, so in fact $H$ has no cycle of length $\le 2k$, i.e. girth$(H)>2k$.

Let $n_H=|V(H)|\le n$, and $d=2e(H)/n_H$ be the average degree of $H$. By repeatedly deleting vertices of degree $<d/2$, we get a nonempty subgraph $H'\subseteq H$ with minimum degree

$$
\delta(H')\ge d/2.
$$

Also girth$(H')>2k$.

Pick $v\in V(H')$ and run **BFS**: levels $L_0=\{v\},L_1,\dots,L_k$. Because girth is $>2k$, the ball of radius $k$ is tree-like, hence

$$
|L_1|\ge \delta,\qquad
|L_i|\ge (\delta-1)|L_{i-1}| \ (2\le i\le k),
$$

where $\delta=\delta(H')$. Therefore

$$
n\ge n_H\ge |V(H')|
\ge 1+\delta\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(\delta-1)^i
\ge (\delta-1)^k.
$$

So $\delta\le n^{1/k}+1$, and thus

$$
d\le 2\delta\le 2n^{1/k}+2.
$$

Hence

$$
e(H)=\frac{dn_H}{2}\le \frac{dn}{2}\le n^{1+1/k}+n.
$$

Finally,

$$
m\le 2e(H)\le 2n^{1+1/k}+2n\le 4n^{1+1/k}.
$$

So we may take $c_k=4$ and has

$$
ex\!\left(n,\mathcal{C}_{\le 2k}^{e}\right)\le c_k n^{1+1/k}.
$$

∎
